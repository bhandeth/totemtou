The Ridge

Before the days,
the place.

Khopra is the trail people who have already walked Annapurna come back for. It is quieter, higher, and shaped differently — most of it spent on a single ridge, with the two big eight-thousanders of the western Himalaya on either side.

What follows is the frame the walk sits inside: the geography, the people, the lake at the end, and the village that built the homestays you sleep in. Read it before the days. The days will read differently.

i.

Between two eight-thousanders

Annapurna I rises to 8,091 m on your right; Dhaulagiri I to 8,167 m on your left. They sit forty-odd kilometres apart, and the trail to Khopra runs along the ridge that separates them. Both peaks have killed more climbers than most. From the ridge you walk between them as if neither existed for anyone but you.

Most of the walking is on this ridge: a long, exposed spine offering 360-degree views — Dhaulagiri, Annapurna, Tukuche, Nilgiri, Hiunchuli, Machhapuchhre. Almost no other trek in Nepal puts both giants in the same frame.

ii.

The community that owns the lodges

Beyond Ghandruk there is no proper village settlement on the trail. The teahouses along the ridge are built and run by a community trust. The homestays at Khopra Ridge are the clearest expression of it — they were established by the local community, and the major share of the profit goes to the local school's development.

You eat dal bhat in a kitchen whose ceiling beams someone in the village cut. Your bed money funds the school down the hill. The economics of the trek are visible.

iii.

Two cultures, one ridge

The trail crosses two ethnic homelands. The lower stretches, through Ghandruk and Tadapani, are Gurung country — terraced barley, slate-roofed houses, the same Gurung who built the ABC trail. Higher up, past Khopra, you descend into Magar territory: Swanta, Banskharka, the farms above Tatopani. Two languages, two cuisines, one continuous walk.

Above the settlements, the ridge is shepherd country in summer. In Saun and Bhadau (July–August) the upper pastures hold flocks of sheep. The trail you are walking is, first, theirs.

iv.

Khayer Lake, the holy water

The lake at 4,660 m is sacred to both Hindus and Buddhists. It is small — you can walk around it in twenty minutes — and it is dark, slate-grey, almost still even in wind. In Shrawan (July–August), pilgrims walk up from villages many days away to bathe in it; the trail is busiest then with people who are not trekkers.

For the rest of the year it sits in silence under the eastern wall of Annapurna South. The walk up is the longest day of the trek. The walk back is a different person walking back.

The Days

Day by day, walked.

The frame above runs underneath all of them. Each day below tells you only what that day adds — what changes underfoot, where the ridge reveals something new, what the mountains do as you climb between them.

01Pokhara → Tadapani

Drive in, walk into the forest. Gurung country, last village.

Drive2–2.5hrs
Walk~6km
Time3hrs
Ascent+690m
Sleep at2,630m

The drive from Pokhara climbs past Nayapul and follows the Modi Khola to Ghandruk — about two and a half hours, the last hour on rough road. Ghandruk is a Gurung village built across a steep slope: stone houses, slate roofs, terraced barley, the kind of place people end multi-day treks at, not start them.

From Ghandruk the trail climbs through oak and rhododendron forest to Tadapani. It is a short day on purpose. The body has just stepped off a road; let it walk for three hours and stop. Tadapani means 'far water' — the village sits on a saddle, with views of Annapurna South and Machhapuchhre on a clear evening.

Place — Ghandruk

The end of the road, the start of the trail

Ghandruk is the last village settlement you will see in this form for several days. Past Tadapani, the trail moves through teahouse settlements built only for trekkers — small clusters along the ridge. The village is also one of the largest Gurung settlements in Nepal, and the source of much of the trail's portering and hospitality tradition.

02Tadapani → Dobato

Off the main trail. Forest, silence, the first 3,400 m.

Walk~9km
Time5–6hrs
Ascent+800m
Descent−10m

Today the route diverges from the busy ABC trail and turns into the forest. The path is narrower, the foot traffic almost nothing — a few trekkers, a few woodcutters, occasional shepherds moving stock between pastures. Oak and rhododendron at first, then taller stands where the canopy closes overhead.

The climb is steady rather than steep. By mid-afternoon you reach Dobato, a tiny cluster of teahouses on a forested shoulder at 3,420 m. The view at sunset, weather permitting, is the whole western Annapurna wall — Annapurna South and Hiunchuli straight ahead, Machhapuchhre to the east, Dhaulagiri starting to show on the far western horizon.

Place — the off-beat trail

Why nobody else is here

The Khopra trail was developed in the early 2010s as a community alternative to the saturated ABC route. It sits one ridge over and one valley west, but for the most part it carries a tenth of the traffic. Past Tadapani, you may pass two or three other small groups in a day, or none.

03Dobato → Muldai → Chistibung

Sunrise on the ridge. Dhaulagiri, finally.

Walk~10km
Time5hrs
Ascent+200m
Descent−570m
Sleep at3,050m
StayTeahouse

An early start — head torches at 4:30 a.m., out of the teahouse by five. The Muldai View Point is a thirty- to forty-minute climb up the ridge above Dobato, on a small singletrack that emerges above the treeline. You arrive in time to see the first light hit Dhaulagiri to the west and the Annapurnas to the east, both at once.

Back to Dobato for breakfast. Then the trail traverses through Bayeli — an alpine meadow used as summer pasture — and gradually descends to Chistibung, a small teahouse settlement at 3,050 m. The day is shorter on the legs than yesterday but the altitude has begun to matter; pacing now starts to matter more than effort.

From Muldai both eight-thousanders are visible at the same sunrise, on opposite horizons. Almost no other point in Nepal does this.
Place — Muldai View Point

3,637 m, on a small singletrack at sunrise

Muldai is one of the major reasons this trail exists in its current form. From a small bare ridge above Dobato, the eastern horizon holds the Annapurna massif — Annapurna South, Hiunchuli, Machhapuchhre, Annapurna I behind — and the western horizon holds Dhaulagiri I and II. The first ten minutes after sunrise are the kind people carry around for years.

04Chistibung → Khopra Ridge

Onto the spine. Dhaulagiri overhead, Annapurna behind.

Walk~6km
Time3–4hrs
Ascent+610m
Descent—
Sleep at3,660m
StayCommunity lodge

A short day in distance, all of it uphill. The trail leaves Chistibung and climbs out of the forest onto open hillside, then onto the ridge proper. By the time you arrive at Khopra you are walking on a long, exposed spine with Dhaulagiri's south face filling the western sky and the Annapurna wall closing the eastern view.

The community lodges at Khopra Ridge are the place you have been climbing toward. Drop your pack. Drink water. Walk fifteen minutes further along the ridge to where the prayer flags sit and the view extends without anything in front of it. Sunset on Dhaulagiri from here is the reason most people remember the trek the way they do.

Place — the homestays

Built by the community, profit to the school

The lodges at Khopra Ridge were established by the local community as a cooperative. The major part of the profit funds the local school down the hill. They are simple — shared rooms, dal bhat from a single kitchen, a wood stove in the dining hall — but they are clean, warm, and the only thing on the ridge. The system works because everyone walking through pays the same as anywhere else, and most of the money stays in the valley.

05Khopra → Khayer Lake → Khopra

The long day. Up to the holy lake, back before dark.

Walk~16km
Time8–10hrs
Ascent+1,000m
Descent−1,000m
High point4,660m
StayKhopra · same lodge

Out of the lodge by five, head torches on, packs light — water, layers, snacks, the medical kit with the lead guide. The trail leaves Khopra and traverses west along the ridge before turning north and climbing through scree and grass toward the lake. The air thins quickly past 4,000 m. Pacing is everything; effort is nothing.

Khayer sits at 4,660 m in a small cirque under the eastern wall of Annapurna South. It is dark, almost still, smaller than expected. People sit at the edge for a while and don't say much. The wind drops and picks up. It is the highest point on this trek, and the only point on this trek that holds a place in two religions.

A holy lake, eight hours up, eight hours back, equally important to Hindus and Buddhists.

The descent is slow on purpose. By late afternoon you are back at Khopra; the last hour, light failing, is on the same ridge you watched the sunrise from. Dinner in the lodge. Lights out early. The body has done a real day.

Place — Khayer Lake

The holy water at 4,660 m

Khayer is sacred to both Hindus and Buddhists, and the only major holy lake on the western Annapurna circuit. In Shrawan (mid-July to mid-August) pilgrims walk up from villages many days away to bathe in it; the lake then is busy in a way it never is otherwise. For the rest of the year, you may have it to yourselves for an hour at a stretch.

The hike up and back is the longest single day on this trek. Most groups start at 5 a.m. to give themselves margin on either end.

Place — between two giants

Annapurna right, Dhaulagiri left

The walk from Khopra to Khayer is, in geographic terms, the most exposed point of this trek. The ridge falls away on both sides into the catchments of two of the world's fourteen eight-thousanders. From the lake, you can see the south face of Annapurna I directly to the east, and the eastern shoulder of Dhaulagiri to the west. The river that drains between them is the Kali Gandaki, and you will exit through it on Day 7.

06Khopra Ridge → Swanta

Down the spine, into Magar country. Pastures, terraces, a different kitchen.

Walk~8km
Time4–5hrs
Ascent—
Descent−1,460m
Sleep at2,200m
StayVillage homestay

The trail leaves Khopra and drops westward off the ridge, through pastures where shepherds move stock between summer and winter ground, then through forest, and eventually out into farmed terraces above Swanta. The descent is steady — fourteen hundred metres in five hours — and easier on the knees than the equivalent descent off ABC because the gradient is gentler.

Swanta is a working Magar village. Stone houses, slate roofs, terraced corn and millet, water wheels on the irrigation channels. The cuisine shifts: you will eat a Magar dal bhat tonight, distinct from the Gurung version you ate higher up. The jeep for the morning is arranged tonight.

Place — Swanta village

Where the trail meets the road again

Swanta is the first proper village settlement since Ghandruk. After three days on a ridge built almost entirely for trekkers, the noise of a village — children, livestock, somebody's radio — is itself a marker of return. The road in from Tatopani is rough; vehicles arrive and leave according to weather and demand.

07Swanta → Pokhara

Out, through the deepest gorge in the world.

Drive5–6hrs
Walk—
Ascent—
Descent−1,380m
Arrive822m
StayHostel

Jeep from Swanta — rough road for the first two hours through Banskharka and on to Tatopani, where the road meets the highway in the Kali Gandaki valley. From Tatopani, the drive south to Pokhara follows the gorge of the Kali Gandaki for much of its length. By the standard measure (river to ridgeline) this is the deepest gorge in the world: the river runs at around 2,500 m below the summits of Annapurna I and Dhaulagiri I on either side, both above 8,000 m.

You arrive in Pokhara by afternoon or early evening. The mountains you walked between are visible from the lakeside. Last dinner together. Goodbyes the next morning.

Place — Kali Gandaki

The river between two eight-thousanders

The Kali Gandaki cuts the gap between Dhaulagiri and Annapurna and is, by the geographer's measure, the deepest gorge in the world. It is older than the Himalaya — the river predates the mountains and has been cutting down through them as they rose. The drive out follows it from Tatopani to where it joins the Madi and the road turns east toward Pokhara.

Is this for you?

A grade, and an honest answer.

We grade our journeys on a 1–6 scale. Khopra sits at four: a step up from ABC. The altitude is higher, the test day longer, the trail less forgiving.

Level 4 — Moderate–ChallengingLong day at altitude, real ridge walking 1 → 6

The walk is not technical. There is no scrambling, no fixed rope, no glacier travel. What it asks is sustained, day-after-day effort with one long, hard day at altitude — Day 5, the round trip to Khayer Lake and back, eight to ten hours, with the high point at 4,660 m.

The two demanding days are Day 4 and Day 5. Day 4 puts you on the ridge at 3,660 m for the first time. Day 5 is the test: a thousand metres of ascent and a thousand back, on a single day, with most of the work above 4,000 m. If you have not been above 4,000 m before, this is where you will find out how your body holds.

The descents on Days 6 and 7 are gentler than the equivalent descents off ABC, but they are still long. Trekking poles help. So does pacing it slow.

If you're ready, you can —
  • Walk 5 km in under 45 minutes without stopping
  • Climb fifteen flights of stairs without your breath catching for long
  • Carry a 5–7 kg daypack for a full day
  • Be eight weeks out from the departure with time to train

Logistics  ·  Safety

The medical kit on the trail

Carried by the lead guide every day. Re-stocked between departures. Used rarely; designed to be ready when it isn't. The kit on Khopra carries a slightly larger oxygen reserve than ABC because Day 5 spends longer above 4,000 m.

  • Portable oxygen cylinder
  • Pulse oximeter
  • Blood pressure monitor
  • Stethoscope
  • Folding stretcher
  • AMS protocol kit (Diamox, Dexamethasone)
  • Trauma kit · splints, dressings, sutures
  • Standard medications · GI, pain, allergy
  • Hand-warmers, emergency bivy
  • Satellite communicator

If something doesn't go to plan

Three of the failure modes we plan for, and how the day works when they happen.

  • Weather closes the route to Khayer Lake We hold at Khopra for an additional day. If the window doesn't open, we walk the ridge for short acclimatisation and exit on schedule. The lake is the goal; getting back is the priority.
  • Altitude affects a member of the group The lead guide makes the call. Mild AMS at Khopra: rest day, hydration, monitoring, no Khayer attempt. Moderate or worsening: descent to Swanta, no negotiation. The kit covers stabilisation; the trail covers the rest.
  • The Swanta jeep is delayed or unavailable The road from Swanta is rough until Tatopani; afternoon jeeps are not always guaranteed. We schedule the morning, hold a backup driver in Tatopani, and if both fail we walk the additional hour to a more reliable pickup. Day 7 is built with that flex in it.
